# Tamil Nadu farmer information-search study

Source: Babu, S.C., Glendenning, C.J., Asenso-Okyere, K., and Govindarajan, S.K. (2012), *Farmers’ Information Needs and Search Behaviors: Case Study in Tamil Nadu, India*, IFPRI Discussion Paper 01165, https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/126226/files/Farmers%20information%20needs%20and%20search%20behaviour%20case%20study%20in%20Tamil%20Nadu%20India.pdf; accessed 2026-08-13. The study is older and focused on rice-oriented districts, so it is not local Namakkal validation.

Relevant findings from the extracted full text: Farmers were heterogeneous in information search behavior. Four clusters were identified—high, medium, semi-medium, and low searchers. The study reports that low searchers relied more on interpersonal sources such as input dealers, state agriculture extension workers, family/relatives, progressive farmers, and cooperative banks, while higher-search groups used more varied print, broadcast, and electronic media. Farmer focus groups identified own experience, input dealers, neighbors/relatives, and agricultural officers as important sources.

The study records specific trust and workflow failures: input dealers were criticized for using farmers’ time pressure to push products, for representing one company and making price comparison difficult; extension staff were criticized for infrequent visits, selective interaction with progressive farmers, and inconsistent or untrustworthy information. At the same time, agricultural assistant officers were sometimes named as preferred sources, showing that trust is relational and performance-dependent rather than simply institutional.

The reported reasons for choosing information sources were proximity (33.7%), assured quality (21.1%), only available option (20.6%), and timely availability (13.7%). Reasons for not using sources included lack of availability (68.4%), not knowing about the source (16.2%), poor service (9.2%), and low relevance (3.0%). The most common information types received were plant protection (28.2%), overall crop information (22.0%), cultivation practices (14.2%), government subsidies (8.9%), and credit (6.0%). In the rice context, disease/pest management and pesticide/fertilizer application were among the most important needs.

Product implication: EcoFarm should test whether a bounded workflow improves a decision in a specific time window, not whether farmers say they want digital advice. It should expect multi-source verification and should not attempt to displace trusted people without evidence. The study’s age, rice focus, and geography limit direct transfer to Namakkal groundnut farmers.
